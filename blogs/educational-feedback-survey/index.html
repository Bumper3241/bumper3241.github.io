Ensuring the Ethicality and Efficacy of an Educational Feedback Survey

More and more in the modern internet age, it’s becoming an increasing necessity to obtain the data of consumers in numerous different fields. From social media and entertainment, to education and professional environments, it seems like nearly everything needs some sort of way to monitor your habits.

That’s not to say that all data monitoring is bad, there are plenty of useful ways that collecting consumer data can directly benefit a product or service’s quality for said consumers. Ranging from more simple ways, like monitoring what videos users of a video platform watch to recommend them similar content they may like, to collecting feedback from users to improve the quality of an online service.

That’s the case of one education institute employed onto their students; before the completion of a course, students are required to complete a survey on the course in order for the school to determine things that need to be changed or updated. On the surface, this seems like a harmless and relatively unintrusive way to garner necessary information; after all, the surveys are promised to be anonymous.

One question from the survey in question.

However, upon further inspection, ethical issues rear their heads with how the survey is conducted. It’s my responsibility here to attempt to address these issues, and offer solutions to them to ensure that the necessary information is still preserved.

The problems at hand and their solutions

From what we’re able to observe, a few issues are able to be seen with how the survey is conducted. Whilst promising anonymity, this can easily be extinguished with minor prying. Firstly, students are required to sign into the portal to access the survey with their school credentials, from there the survey link is active for a limited period of time.

This almost immediately removes the “Anonymous” nature of the survey. By requiring the students to use traceable login information, this, whether used by school faculty or not, must be stored somewhere, and therefore could hypothetically be accessed. On top of this, questions that delve into more specific details could be traced back to students who spoke about these issues during class, however this is much harder of a factor to address, and will not be taken into account for solutions.

The most simple solution is to remove the login process. The survey link is already extremely limited in its access period. By removing the login process, this can instill a sense of safety within students, and fully anonymizes the survey’s results, ensuring that students can’t be traced to their replies.

The obvious concern then becomes how to prevent abuse. Removing a sign in page can potentially open the survey up to be abused by numerous answers, however the solution to this is almost even easier. Storing a cookie within the browser that tells the survey site that this survey has already been completed is a simple solution that should prevent multiple answers from the same student.

Solving the privacy problem also solves another ethical problem of the survey as well—retaliation. If a student has a grievance with a professor or the course itself, this survey offers a way to honestly air the grievances out, however if the responses can be traced to a student, they could face repercussions or less than favourable treatment from a professor or school faculty that may affect their tenure at the school. Whilst it’s the student’s responsibility not to include extremely specific information that someone with knowledge of their experience in class could trace them with, removing the credentials ensures genuine anonymity, allowing more honest criticism and feedback from students.

Of course, these solutions require a lot of backend restructuring. Management may be looking to cut corners, and if insisting that these solutions are too drastic in the way that they change the way data is sourced, the only other way I could see moving forward ethically is to have staff sign agreements to not snoop through data, though this is a much less secure band-aid solution that relies on the honesty of school faculty.

Overall, the information discussed can be simplified as follows.

Current privacy concerns

  • Requiring traceable login information to access the survey.
  • Possibility of retaliation from faculty that dislike the feedback a student provided.
  • Identifiable information may be able to be included within the survey.

Proposed solutions

  • Removing the requirement for a student login in place of a cookie that disallows multiple completions of the survey.
  • Require staff to sign a document disallowing them from speaking to students regarding their answers on the survey.

I believe that it is entirely possible to be able to source this type of data ethically. This survey simply highlights common shortfalls that might not have even crossed the minds of the management figures who implemented it with good intentions.
