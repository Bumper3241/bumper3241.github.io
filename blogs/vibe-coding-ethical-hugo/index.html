Vibe-Coding is Changing the Gaming Space. Is it Ethical?

Seemingly more and more frequently, you’re hearing about AI’s role in projects all across the web. One way this has taken form is the gaming space. Seemingly every day, you’re hearing about projects that seem more and more insane. From the technically impressive world of decompilations—that is, reverse-engineering the source code of older games to allow them to be more easily understood by end-users and enable them to run natively on modern hardware or platforms they were never intended for—to insane modding projects, like the several different games that have Minecraft mechanics plopped in, allowing Steve to place and build houses and blow up Creepers in the likes of Skyrim, Grand Theft Auto, and Elden Ring.

Each one, whilst seeming harmless on the surface, save for the insane social media numbers they generate, is actually an ethical dilemma in disguise. All of these projects have their origins linked to a relatively recent roll-out of Claude’s Sonnet 4.6. This is seeming to be one of the smartest consumer-level AI models ever seen. Within minutes, it was able to analyze the source code of seemingly anything that you gave it (though recently, it’s seemingly like Anthropic has manually disabled the ability for Claude to rebuild already existing games).

Along with the renaissance of classic titles that you might remember if you grew up in the 2000s like I did being exploited in ways never thought imaginable, such as the PC port of Sony’s Dark Cloud series, a previously exclusive title to the PlayStation 2, to online multiplayer versions of EA’s Need for Speed: Underground 2 with hundreds of players able to interact with each other in a 20-year-old, now-defunct title, comes a slew of questions and ethical issues that beg the simple question of “Is this really okay?”

I’d like to try and tackle each of these within this post and see if we can still try to find enjoyment in older video games being given brand-new life.

Copyright Issues

We’ll begin with what is the easiest issue to dispute—does this all not violate some sort of copyright law?

The simple answer is: No, it doesn’t.

This is due simply to how decompilation projects as a whole are created and distributed. Whilst piracy is a key concern, the simple fact is that, much like emulators, no actual protected files are being handed out. It’s up to you yourself to obtain a legal copy of the game you’re wanting to play or disassemble. Of course, there’s absolutely nothing stopping you from searching “Super Mario ROM” in Google and finding dozens of sketchy websites ready to mangle your computer, but that’s your own prerogative. Most people, including myself, own the games and have the means to rip their files relatively easily (you can literally insert a PS2 game into your disk drive and copy the ISO; it’s that easy).

From there, an AI agent is able to run through the file and assist in building it from scratch, though of course, ideally you’ll have a background in a language yourself, though we’ll tackle that issue coming up. The GitHub pages for these decompilation projects will almost never have these real game files in them, lest they be subject to legal action from multi-billion-dollar companies.

AI Disclosure

Of course, it’s ethically necessary to disclose that you’re using AI in these projects. In the past, coding languages were previously exclusive through obscurity, requiring the drive to learn to use, which is what made coding and computer engineering jobs so prestigious. In our modern world, however, this exclusivity has been made somewhat obsolete, as anyone is able to just ask an AI agent to code for them. Though I am personally morally opposed to almost any AI usage via LLMs (aside from converting to Markdown, all of my writing is 100% my own), some people aren’t, and that’s okay. Primarily, it is important to disclose AI usage for the sake of transparency.

The Flooding of Vibe-Coded Slop

Another important ethical issue regarding AI is the slurry of awful, buggy programs created by someone with zero experience in coding being let loose on the internet. Time and time again, a game is released on low-barrier platforms like Steam or Roblox, and as it turns out, one single guy who’s barely technically literate has created something that barely functions, but is already monetized. The natural consequence of this is game platforms, such as Itch.io, having a flood of low-quality games that nobody plays, taking up storage space to the benefit of absolutely nobody.

Very luckily, some of these platforms are naturally adapting to the market demands of players who want real games. Steam and Itch.io have NoAI tags that allow users to choose to filter out games that have large amounts of AI usage in the development.

The Natural End-Goal

Up until this point, I’ve tried to keep this a relatively light-hearted discussion, keeping it relevant to gaming as a hobby. But that’s not where this ends. While it’s cool to see somebody playing Minecraft in GTA, or to be able to play 20-year-old games in a web browser with 1,000 people, this doesn’t stop at video games.

It’s a somewhat well-known fact that most infrastructural software is horrendously out-of-date. Stuff like banks, government services, and other web-based services use old software that is updated only semi-frequently, and is kept secure through only a few means of encryption.

Once again, this was fine for what it was. It would take ages to figure out the encryption keys of a small bank’s web service, and for hardly any benefit, it was security through obfuscation. But now? It’s possible to have a gang of AI agents brute-force something like that within only a couple of days.

The end-point is that web security needs an immense overhaul. We’ve already seen security problems rear their heads just a couple of weeks ago, such as the PHP exploits, or almost any Linux host being able to be remotely controlled. These bugs were discovered with AI agents, and were only not used in the past just due to how obscure they were.

Conclusion

It’s almost impossible to say what the future of the internet will look like with incredibly advanced AI agents leading huge projects like these. Whether or not they’re used for hobby projects or cybersecurity breaches, it’s important to know the ethical concerns of them and proceed accordingly.
